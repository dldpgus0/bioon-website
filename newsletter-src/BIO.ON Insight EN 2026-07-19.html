🔤 Word of the Week

Complete Response Letter

CRL (Complete Response Letter)

The key term behind this week’s story on the FDA resuming publication of rejection letters (Story 2)

A CRL (complete response letter) is the formal rejection letter the FDA sends a drug company when it doesn’t approve a new drug application. It spells out what must be fixed to win approval (more clinical data, manufacturing improvements, labelling changes and so on). Importantly, a CRL isn’t a ‘permanent rejection’ but a letter asking the company to ‘fix and resubmit’. Even so, share prices often plunge right after a CRL is announced — and the contents of these letters are now starting to be made public.

💡 Analogy: An FDA CRL is like the ‘revisions required’ letter a thesis committee sends. You didn’t pass, but it tells you specifically why, where and what more you need to do. Until now this letter was a secret between the company and the FDA — and now it’s starting to be made public.

Before CRL publication — only the company’s account

The drug company disclosed the FDA’s reasons for rejection itself. Sensitive issues such as safety concerns, missing data or manufacturing problems were softened as much as possible or left out. For investors and patients, it was hard to know the real reason.

After CRL publication — the FDA’s original text

The FDA’s letter itself (with some confidential parts redacted) is published. It shows specifically which data was lacking and which safety signal was the problem. Companies find it harder to ‘spin’ the reasons for rejection, and investors, competitors and patients all have the same information.

📰 This Week’s Top 3 Stories

Story 1 · Alzheimer’s

Biogen’s Tau-Targeting Drug Diranersen Heads Straight to Phase 3 Despite Missing Its Primary Endpoint — a New Bar for Alzheimer’s Science

BioSpace · Investing.com · Biogen (NASDAQ: BIIB) | AAIC 2026 · 2026.07.14

Key Facts

According to Phase 2 data Biogen presented at AAIC 2026 in London, its tau-targeting drug diranersen cut toxic tau protein tangles in the brain by 50–65%. But the primary clinical endpoint, measuring cognitive improvement, did not reach statistical significance. Even so, Biogen decided to move into Phase 3, citing positive signals across secondary measures and the biological meaning of tau reduction. Where existing Alzheimer’s drugs (Leqembi and others) focus on clearing ‘amyloid plaques (the rubbish),’ diranersen goes directly after the ‘tau tangles’ that actually kill brain cells — a more fundamental approach in terms of mechanism.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

An Alzheimer’s brain has two kinds of protein abnormality: ① amyloid plaques that build up outside cells, and ② tau protein that tangles inside cells. Existing drugs focused on cleaning up ①, but it’s ② that actually kills brain cells. Biogen’s diranersen attacks ② directly. It missed the primary endpoint — but the fact that it cut tau by more than half is scientifically valid evidence in itself, and Biogen and the market read it as ‘progress,’ not ‘failure.’

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

Korea now has over a million people living with dementia, and given how fast the population is ageing, the Alzheimer’s drug market in Korea is set to grow explosively. As the tau-targeting approach looks more promising, Korean drug developers (Medifron, Abion and others) and researchers have more incentive to strengthen tau and synapse pipelines. Growing numbers of Korean research presentations at global neuroscience conferences like AAIC are another trend worth watching.

Story 2 · FDA Transparency

The FDA Resumes Publishing Drug Rejection Letters (CRLs) — Information Drug Companies Kept Hidden Comes Out

Fierce Biotech · FDA | 2026.07.14

Key Facts

The FDA resumed its policy of publishing complete response letters (CRLs), which it had briefly paused in April 2026 after legal pushback from Big Pharma law firms. A CRL is the formal letter the FDA sends when it rejects a new drug application, detailing the reasons (safety concerns, missing data, manufacturing defects and so on). Until now these letters were confidential between the company and the FDA, and companies could shape their disclosures in a favourable light, such as ‘we plan to submit additional data.’ Under the publication policy, investors, patients and competitors can all read the FDA’s original text, making it harder for companies to ‘spin’ the reasons for rejection — which could mean bigger share-price drops after a rejection and a higher risk of securities lawsuits.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Until now, when the FDA rejected a drug, the company’s disclosure would say something like ‘we plan to resubmit with additional data.’ The specific reasons were hidden. Now the FDA itself publishes the letter with the reasons. The exam score is the same, but now everyone can see which questions were wrong and why — a far more transparent, and more uncomfortable, world for companies.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

It directly affects Korean companies filing new drugs with the US FDA (Hanmi, Yuhan, Chong Kun Dang and others). If a CRL’s contents are made public, IR and disclosure strategy will need to be far more carefully planned. Korea’s MFDS may also look to the FDA policy on transparency, so in the long run, communication around new drug approvals in Korea could change too.

📌 The FDA has resumed publishing drug rejection letters — when a company gets a CRL, the reasons are now visible to investors and patients. The balance of information is shifting. — Summary of Fierce Biotech

Story 3 · Oral Obesity Pill · Coverage

IQVIA Prescription Tracking for Foundayo — Market Dominance Is Decided by Formularies, Not Science

Fierce Pharma · IQVIA data · Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) | Jul 17, 2026

Key Facts

According to real prescription-tracking data compiled by market research firm IQVIA, Lilly’s oral obesity pill Foundayo is trailing competitors badly in its early prescription trajectory. The key cause cited isn’t efficacy but delayed entry onto major PBMs’ insurance formularies. Injectable GLP-1s (Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound) already have established coverage routes, but as a new oral molecule, Foundayo has to go through separate PBM negotiations and listing. It proves once again that dominance in the US obesity drug market depends more on PBM negotiating power than on clinical data.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

IQVIA data tracks the number of actual pharmacy prescriptions in real time. Investors judge a new drug’s performance in this order: FDA approval → IQVIA prescription data → sales forecasts. Few Foundayo prescriptions means ‘prescriptions aren’t reaching pharmacies,’ and the reason is ‘PBMs haven’t listed it, so it isn’t covered, so patients and doctors hold back.’ Even the best drug won’t make it onto the prescription pad if it loses at the pricing table.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

In Korea, the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) plays a role similar to a PBM — without reimbursement listing, patient costs rise sharply and prescribing is limited. With coverage for oral GLP-1 obesity drugs in Korea still uncertain, Foundayo can expect the same channel barrier if it launches here. It’s why demand is growing for Market Access specialists and health insurance negotiation strategists.

📌 Real IQVIA prescription data confirms Foundayo’s slow start — the winner in the US obesity market isn’t the best science, but whoever wins the PBM negotiation. — Summary of Fierce Pharma

🔬 This Week’s Deep Dive

Deep Dive · How Should You Read Clinical Data?

A missed primary endpoint → straight to Phase 3,
rejection letters made public, slow prescriptions — there’s more than one measure of success

① Same data, different readings

This week’s three stories each show, from a different angle, how complex ‘the measure of success in biotech’ is. Biogen missed its primary endpoint, yet the market welcomed Phase 3. The FDA is publishing information companies used to control (CRLs), making it harder to ‘dress things up as success.’ Foundayo won in the clinic, but IQVIA prescription data shows the channel failure in numbers. If you only look at the clinical result, you will get it wrong — you need the context for interpreting the data too.

② Measures of success in three stories — compared

How to read it

③ Why this distinction matters — layers of information

When you read biotech news, look at three layers together. ① The scientific layer — not just the primary endpoint, but the biological mechanism and the secondary measures as a whole. ② The regulatory and disclosure layer — the gap between the company’s disclosure and the FDA’s original text (CRL publication narrows this gap). ③ The market layer — IQVIA prescription data, PBM listing and real patient access. The people who read all three layers at once are the truly valuable analysts in biotech.

④ Career takeaway — data interpretation is your competitive edge

✓ Skill to build

The ability to read clinical data on multiple layers. You need practice combining the primary endpoint result + changes in biological biomarkers + IQVIA prescription tracking + published CRL content to judge ‘where does this drug actually stand?’ Medical Affairs, Clinical Development, Market Access and Regulatory Affairs roles all demand this multi-layered reading.

⚠ Trap

The simple reading of ‘missed primary endpoint = this drug won’t work.’ As Biogen showed, even a missed primary endpoint can lead to Phase 3 if the biological signal is strong enough. Conversely, even an FDA-approved drug may not get prescribed (Foundayo). Drop the habit of judging by the one-line headline of a clinical result — look at the methodology, biomarkers and market context together.

💡 Editor’s Insight

If I sum up this week in one sentence: ‘Clinical data is a starting point, not a conclusion.’ Biogen missed its primary endpoint but the science moved forward, the FDA shook companies’ monopoly on information, and Foundayo showed the reality of channels in IQVIA numbers. To read biotech properly, you need to see the context beyond the one-line clinical result — the biological signal, the regulatory original, the real prescription data.

🔤 Word of the Week

Antibody-Drug Conjugate

ADC (Antibody-Drug Conjugate)

The key technology behind this week’s GSK and Hansoh survival win in small cell lung cancer Phase 3 (Story 2)

An ADC (antibody-drug conjugate) is a cancer drug that links two things into one: ① an antibody (the guided missile) that finds cancer cells precisely, chemically linked (conjugated) to ② a powerful cytotoxic payload (the warhead) that kills them. When the antibody recognises and binds a specific protein on the cancer cell surface, the toxin is released inside, selectively destroying only cancer cells. Risvutatug rezetecan, which GSK and Hansoh proved extends survival in Phase 3, is an ADC targeting the B7-H3 protein — the first Phase 3 survival data for this protein, which is overexpressed in many cancer types.

💡 Analogy: Traditional chemotherapy is a ‘bomber’ — it hits normal cells near the cancer too. An ADC is a ‘smart missile’ — it flies with the cancer cell’s address (the antibody) and explodes only at that exact house (the cancer cell). The core promise of ADCs is stronger anti-cancer effect with fewer side effects like hair loss, nausea and weakened immunity.

Traditional chemotherapy

Cytotoxic drugs are given into the bloodstream to attack rapidly dividing cells. They kill cancer cells, but normal cells such as hair, gut and immune cells are hit too — the main cause of hair loss, nausea and weakened immunity.

ADC (antibody-drug conjugate)

The antibody finds and binds the cancer cell, and the toxin is released only inside it. Far less toxin reaches normal tissue, improving the side-effect profile. This is how GSK and Hansoh’s ADC became the first to prove longer survival in Phase 3 in small cell lung cancer, where five-year survival is only about 7%.

📰 This Week’s Top 3 Stories

Story 1 · Oral Obesity Pill

Lilly’s Oral Obesity Pill Foundayo Off to a Slow First Month — Held Back by Insurance Coverage Barriers

Fierce Pharma · Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) | 2026.07.10

Key Facts

According to IQVIA prescription data, prescriptions for Eli Lilly’s newly launched oral obesity drug Foundayo are growing more slowly than for Novo Nordisk’s competing product. The cause isn’t efficacy but delays in coverage by PBMs (pharmacy benefit managers). In the US, the big three PBMs — Express Scripts, CVS Caremark and OptumRx — manage about 80% of all prescriptions, and Foundayo isn’t yet listed widely enough on their formularies, so patients get it without insurance benefits or doctors hold back from prescribing it. It’s the product that should open ‘the needle-free obesity drug era,’ but without control of the insurance channel, prescriptions don’t come.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

In the US, a prescription moves from the doctor → the pharmacy → the PBM. A PBM has to list a drug (‘it’s on our formulary’) before patients can buy it at the insured price. An unlisted drug is fully out-of-pocket, so prescriptions dry up. If a good drug gets pushed down the formulary, it can lose the market to a more ordinary competitor — which is exactly where Foundayo is now.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

Korea has a similar structure — new drugs not listed for national health insurance reimbursement carry high out-of-pocket costs, which limits prescribing. If Korean reimbursement talks for oral GLP-1s are delayed, Foundayo’s impact in Korea may be limited too. It’s why demand is growing in Korea for Market Access (pricing and reimbursement) specialists.

Story 2 · Cancer ADC

GSK and Hansoh’s ADC Extends Survival in Small Cell Lung Cancer Phase 3 — a Historic First Win for B7-H3

Fierce Biotech · GSK (NYSE: GSK) × Hansoh Pharmaceutical | 2026.07.10

Key Facts

Risvutatug rezetecan, an ADC co-developed by GSK and Hansoh Pharmaceutical, met its key survival endpoint in a Phase 3 trial in advanced small cell lung cancer (SCLC). It’s the world’s first ADC targeting the B7-H3 protein to prove longer survival in Phase 3. SCLC has extremely low five-year survival even among lung cancers (about 7%) and limited treatment options — that unmet need is also the strongest market case for the ADC. B7-H3 is overexpressed in many cancers beyond lung cancer, so it has blockbuster potential through expansion to new indications.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

SCLC responds well to chemotherapy at first, but relapses quickly and leaves almost no options afterwards. The ADC uses its antibody to find cancer cells covered in B7-H3 and drops its toxin with precision. A ‘smart missile’ broke through a defensive line the old bombers never could. Phase 3 survival data is the strongest evidence regulators (FDA, EMA) ask for, so it forms the direct basis for a filing.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

Korea’s ADC leader LegoChem Biosciences has grown by licensing its ADC linker and payload technology to global Big Pharma. GSK and Hansoh’s success raises confidence in B7-H3-targeted ADCs overall, a positive signal for Korean ADC companies’ bargaining power in global partnerships. It’s also a good moment to watch Korean companies with ADC pipelines, such as Dong-A ST and ABL Bio.

📌 GSK and Hansoh’s ADC succeeded in extending survival in small cell lung cancer Phase 3 — a historic first win for a B7-H3-targeted ADC. Now the commercial race begins. — Summary of Fierce Biotech

Story 3 · AI Drug Discovery

Xaira Therapeutics Steps Out — a Multi-Billion-Dollar AI Drug Start-Up Goes Looking for Big Pharma Partners

BioSpace · Company briefing · Xaira Therapeutics (private) | Jul 8, 2026

Key Facts

Silicon Valley AI drug discovery start-up Xaira Therapeutics went public-facing for the first time since its founding, saying it is actively exploring co-development partnerships with large pharma companies. Xaira raised over $1B at its founding in 2024, one of the largest initial rounds for a single start-up in AI drug discovery. It uses generative AI models to automate everything from protein structure prediction to drug candidate design. But however good an AI platform is, a drug can’t reach patients without the infrastructure to run Phase 1–3 trials itself (patient recruitment, clinical networks, regulatory experience) — so it’s knocking on Big Pharma’s door.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

An AI company makes a new drug in two stages: ① using AI to design ‘which molecule will act on which protein,’ and ② clinical trials that actually give the molecule to people. ① can be transformed by AI, but ② still needs a decade-long human clinical infrastructure. However brilliant Xaira’s AI is, its drugs can’t reach the market without Big Pharma’s clinical network — this structural dependence is the shared limit of AI drug start-ups.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

Korean AI drug companies (Standigm, Pharos iBio, Syntekabio and others) face the same structural challenge. Even with AI-designed candidates, proving their value is hard without a partner to run the trials. Xaira’s case shows Korean AI drug companies once again that ‘BD (business development) and clinical partnership capability matter as much as the technology platform.’

🔬 This Week’s Deep Dive

Deep Dive · A New Drug’s Three Gates — Science, Channels, Partnerships

The drug is finished —
now the walls of coverage, commercialisation and clinical networks remain

① Three things a new drug must clear to reach patients

This week’s three stories show ‘the second fight’ in different corners of the industry. Foundayo won in the clinic but got stuck in the PBM insurance channel. GSK and Hansoh’s ADC won Phase 3, but now the commercial race begins. Xaira has top-tier AI but no clinical network, so it’s knocking on Big Pharma’s door. In drug development, ‘the science wins’ is only half the story — gates outside the lab, in coverage, channels and partnerships, always remain.

③ Why this distinction matters — gates reveal jobs

The three gates create demand for different specialist roles. The PBM channel fight needs Market Access and drug access specialists, commercialisation after Phase 3 calls for Medical Affairs, Regulatory Affairs (RA) and marketing, and AI platform partnerships are led by BD (business development) and clinical development strategists. Being good at science alone won’t get you through these gates — which is why bioworkers need to understand what happens ‘after the research.’

④ Career takeaway — the path after the science

✓ Skill to build

The ability to understand what happens ‘after the research.’ Market Access (reimbursement and formulary strategy), Medical Affairs (communicating clinical data), BD (partnership deals) and RA (regulatory approval) — these roles own every gate a new drug passes from the lab to the patient. As much as reading clinical data, you need to think about ‘how will this data look to an insurance reviewer?’ and ‘what terms does the partner want?’

⚠ Trap

Thinking ‘a good drug will always sell.’ As Foundayo shows, even the most innovative drug won’t be prescribed if it isn’t covered. An AI-designed candidate won’t reach patients without a clinical partner either. A drug’s value isn’t decided by scientific excellence alone — build the eye to look at market entry strategy, channels and partnerships together.

💡 Editor’s Insight

The thought I had reading this week’s three stories was: ‘Science has already found the answer, but the world isn’t ready yet.’ Foundayo’s efficacy is proven but insurers are blocking it, the ADC won Phase 3 but the commercial race is just beginning, and Xaira’s AI is impressive but can’t go it alone without a clinical network. If you see drug development as a journey ‘from the lab to the patient,’ clinical data is only the halfway point. The people who understand the other half — coverage, commercialisation and partnerships — are the ones who will create real value in biotech.

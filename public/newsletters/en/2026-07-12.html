🔤 Word of the Week

ADC (Antibody-Drug Conjugate)

The technology behind this week's GSK and Hansoh small-cell lung cancer win (Story 2)

An ADC links two things into one cancer drug: an antibody that finds cancer cells precisely (the guided missile) and a powerful cytotoxic payload that kills them (the warhead). The antibody latches onto a specific protein on the cancer cell's surface, and the toxin is released inside, destroying mainly cancer cells. GSK and Hansoh's risvutatug rezetecan targets B7-H3, a protein overexpressed in many cancers, and this is the first Phase 3 survival data for a B7-H3 drug.

💡 Analogy: Traditional chemotherapy is a bomber: it damages healthy cells near the target too. An ADC is a smart missile carrying the cancer cell's address (the antibody) and exploding only at that house. The promise is stronger anti-cancer effect with less hair loss, nausea and immune suppression.

Traditional chemotherapy

Cytotoxic drugs go into the bloodstream and attack any fast-dividing cells. They kill cancer cells but also harm hair, gut and immune cells, which is behind hair loss, nausea and low immunity.

ADC (antibody-drug conjugate)

The antibody finds the cancer cell, and the toxin is released inside it. Healthy tissue sees much less toxin, so side effects improve. GSK and Hansoh's ADC used this approach to extend survival in Phase 3 in small-cell lung cancer, where five-year survival is only around 7%.

📰 This Week's Top 3 Stories

STORY 1 · ORAL OBESITY DRUG · MARKET ACCESS

Lilly's Oral Obesity Pill Foundayo Gets Off to a Slow Start as Insurance Coverage Lags

Fierce Pharma · Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) | Jul 10, 2026

Key Facts

According to IQVIA prescription data, Eli Lilly's newly launched oral obesity drug Foundayo is being prescribed more slowly than Novo Nordisk's competing product. The reason isn't efficacy: it's delays in getting on pharmacy benefit manager (PBM) formularies. In the US, the three big PBMs — Express Scripts, CVS Caremark and OptumRx — manage about 80% of prescriptions. Without broad formulary coverage, patients face full cash prices and doctors hesitate to prescribe. It's a product that could open the "needle-free obesity drug" era, but without the insurance channel, the prescriptions don't come.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

US prescriptions move from doctor to pharmacy to PBM. A drug has to be on a PBM's list for patients to get the insured price; if it isn't, patients pay everything themselves, and prescriptions dry up. A good drug that loses out on formularies can lose the market to a more ordinary rival. That's where Foundayo is right now.

🎓 Career Lens

Most relevant for: Market Access, Pricing & Reimbursement, Commercial Strategy

If you're interviewing for market access, you'll want an example of a strong drug that still struggled at launch. This is it:

"Foundayo's slow start isn't about efficacy. It's about formulary access. In the US, three PBMs manage around 80 percent of prescriptions, so until Foundayo is on their lists, patients face the full price. It shows that launch planning has to start with payers, not just with the clinical data."

Why it matters: This is the day-to-day work of market access teams, and in the US it's increasingly where launches are won or lost.

STORY 2 · ONCOLOGY · ADC

GSK and Hansoh's B7-H3 ADC Extends Survival in Small-Cell Lung Cancer Phase 3, a First for the Target

Fierce Biotech · GSK (NYSE: GSK) × Hansoh Pharmaceutical | Jul 10, 2026

Key Facts

GSK and Hansoh Pharmaceutical's co-developed ADC risvutatug rezetecan met its primary survival endpoint in a Phase 3 trial in advanced small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). It's the first ADC targeting B7-H3 to show a survival benefit in Phase 3. SCLC has a five-year survival rate of about 7% and very few treatment options, and that unmet need is the strongest case for the drug. B7-H3 is overexpressed in many other cancers too, which gives it blockbuster potential through new indications.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

SCLC often responds to chemotherapy at first but comes back quickly, with few options afterwards. The ADC uses an antibody to find cancer cells covered in B7-H3 and drops its toxin precisely. A smart missile got through a line the old bombers never could. Phase 3 survival data is the strongest evidence regulators like the FDA and EMA ask for, so it's the direct basis for a filing.

🎓 Career Lens

Most relevant for: Clinical Development, Medical Affairs, Regulatory Affairs

Oncology interviews often ask about a recent trial you found meaningful. Try:

"GSK and Hansoh's B7-H3 ADC hit its survival endpoint in Phase 3 in small-cell lung cancer, where five-year survival is around 7 percent. It's the first B7-H3 drug to do that. And because B7-H3 shows up in many other tumours, the next question is how many indications it can expand into."

Why it matters: Survival wins trigger filings, launch planning and indication expansion trials, which means more work for clinical, regulatory and medical teams.

STORY 3 · AI DRUG DISCOVERY

Xaira Therapeutics Comes Out of Stealth and Goes Looking for Big Pharma Partners

BioSpace · company briefing · Xaira Therapeutics (private) | Jul 8, 2026

Key Facts

Silicon Valley AI drug discovery startup Xaira Therapeutics made its first public move since launch, saying it is actively seeking co-development partnerships with Big Pharma. Xaira raised more than $1 billion when it was founded in 2024, one of the largest starting war chests for any AI drug startup. It uses generative AI to automate everything from protein structure prediction to candidate design. But even the best AI platform can't get a drug to patients without the infrastructure to run Phase 1–3 trials: patient recruitment, clinical networks and regulatory experience. Hence the knock on Big Pharma's door.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

An AI company making a drug has two steps: (1) designing which molecule will act on which protein, and (2) testing it in people. AI can transform step one, but step two still needs a decade of human clinical infrastructure. However good Xaira's AI is, its drugs can't reach the market without a clinical network, and that dependence is a limit every AI drug startup shares.

🎓 Career Lens

Most relevant for: Business Development, Alliance Management, Clinical Development Strategy

If you're asked what limits AI drug discovery companies, this makes a good concrete example:

"Even with over a billion dollars, Xaira is looking for Big Pharma partners, because AI can speed up discovery but it can't run a Phase 3. The clinical network, the patient recruitment and the regulatory experience still sit with large companies. So for AI biotechs, BD is just as important as the platform."

Why it matters: AI biotechs need BD and alliance people who can sell a platform to partners and structure deals, and it's one of the less obvious ways into the sector.

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · Three Gates Every Drug Must Pass

The drug is done —
now come insurance, commercialisation and clinical networks

① Three things a drug must get past to reach patients

This week's stories show three companies each fighting their second battle. Foundayo won in the clinic but got stuck in the PBM channel. GSK and Hansoh's ADC won Phase 3, and now the commercial race begins. Xaira has top-tier AI but no clinical network, so it's knocking on Big Pharma's door. "The science won" is only half the story in drug development. Insurance, channels and partnerships are waiting outside the lab.

② Three gates, compared
Lilly (channel) GSK × Hansoh (clinical) Xaira (AI)
Where It Stands
Launched · negotiating PBM coverage Phase 3 survival endpoint met Looking for partners
Key Challenge
Getting onto PBM formularies FDA filing and commercialisation Securing a clinical network
Main Risk
Rival (Novo) gets there first Fierce competition among ADCs Unfavourable partnership terms

③ Why the distinction matters — every gate has its own jobs

Each gate creates demand for different specialists. The PBM fight needs market access and reimbursement experts. Commercialising a Phase 3 winner needs medical affairs, regulatory and marketing. AI partnerships need BD and clinical strategy people to make the deals. Being good at science alone won't get a drug through these gates, and that's why understanding what happens after research matters so much.

④ Career takeaway: learn the language after the lab

✓ Skill to build

The thread through all three stories is the same: the value of a drug is decided partly outside the lab. Try reading clinical data the way a payer, a partner or a regulator would. Ask "how would this look to an insurer?" or "what would a partner want in return?" That habit is what market access, medical affairs, BD and RA teams use every day, and it will make you sound like someone who already works in the industry.

⚠ Trap

Believing that a good drug will always sell. Foundayo shows that even an innovative drug won't be prescribed without insurance coverage, and an AI-designed candidate won't reach patients without a clinical partner. Scientific excellence alone doesn't decide a drug's value; look at market entry strategy, channels and partnerships too.

💡 Editor's Insight

What stayed with me this week was the feeling that the science has answered, but the world isn't ready yet. When you first get into biotech, it's easy to think passing Phase 3 is the finish line. But if you look at a drug's journey from lab to patient, clinical data is roughly the halfway point. The people who understand the other half — the language of insurance, channels and partnerships — are the ones who'll create real value in this industry. See you next issue. 🙏

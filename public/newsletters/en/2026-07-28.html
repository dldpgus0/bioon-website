Drugs keep getting more complex —
so why do they keep getting easier for patients?

Lilly’s triple agonist retatrutide cutting weight by 22.6%, J&J’s off-the-shelf bispecific combo strategy,
and Merck building an early supply chain before approval — this week’s three stories, read from a Korean industry angle.
Lining up this week’s news, one question came to mind: “Where do winning biotechs add complexity, and where do they take it away?” Lilly’s retatrutide raised its receptor targets to three to maximise efficacy. J&J’s bispecific combo, by contrast, stripped out CAR-T’s ‘custom manufacturing’ complexity entirely to make an off-the-shelf treatment, and Merck cut a daily pill down to once a month — while laying down its distribution network before approval. 🔤 Word of the Week

Off-the-Shelf

Off-the-Shelf

The core competitive edge of this week’s J&J bispecific combination (Story 2)

Off-the-shelf means a drug that’s already made and sitting in storage can be taken out and given right away, with no need to collect and process each patient’s own cells. CAR-T cell therapy uses a ‘custom manufacturing’ model — collecting the patient’s T cells, genetically modifying them and infusing them back — which usually takes weeks. Bispecific antibodies like Tecvayli and Talvey, on the other hand, are finished medicines mass-produced in a factory, so they can be given as soon as they’re prescribed. The key this week is that J&J achieved CAR-T-level results — an 89% lower risk of progression — with off-the-shelf drugs.

💡 Analogy: CAR-T is a ‘bespoke suit’ tailored to your body — you get measured, it gets cut, and you wait weeks. A bispecific combo is ‘off the rack’ — if the size fits, you wear it on the spot. The bespoke suit may fit better, but for a patient, those weeks can be a matter of life and death.

CAR-T — autologous manufacturing

Personalised manufacturing: collect the patient’s T cells → genetically modify → grow → re-infuse. It usually takes 2–5 weeks, with a risk that the patient’s condition worsens in the meantime. Manufacturing facility and cost barriers are high too.

Bispecific combo — off-the-shelf manufacturing

Finished antibodies mass-produced in a factory, given as soon as they’re prescribed. With no per-patient manufacturing wait, it suits patients who relapse quickly, and supply and distribution are as simple as for ordinary medicines.

📰 This Week’s Top 3 Stories

Story 1 · Obesity & Metabolic Disease

Lilly’s ‘Triple Agonist’ Retatrutide Cuts Weight by Up to 22.6% in Phase 3 — the Generation After GLP-1 Is Coming

Fierce Biotech · Clinical Trials Arena · Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) | 2026.07.23

Key Facts

Eli Lilly released Phase 3 data for its triple agonist candidate retatrutide. In adults with severe obesity treated for 80 weeks, average weight loss reached up to 22.6%. The triple mechanism, activating three receptors at once — GLP-1, GIP and glucagon — increases energy expenditure while suppressing appetite, delivering a stronger effect than existing single- and dual-target drugs. Based on this data, Lilly plans to file with regulators worldwide in early 2027, aiming to cement its leadership in an obesity market expected to top $100B.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Existing drugs like Wegovy and Zepbound target GLP-1 alone, or GLP-1 + GIP. Retatrutide adds the glucagon receptor on top — like bolting on one more engine. More engines make the mechanism more complex, but they also open more routes for fat breakdown and energy use, so weight loss is bigger. It’s a strategy that accepts mechanistic complexity to maximise efficacy.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

Interest in obesity drugs has exploded in Korea since Wegovy and Mounjaro arrived, but without insurance coverage, patients pay a lot out of pocket. If a triple agonist is approved, the price is likely to be even higher, so reimbursement talks will be the key variable when it comes to Korea. Korean companies with metabolic disease pipelines, such as Hanmi and Yuhan, have growing reason to benchmark multi-target strategies too.

Story 2 · Blood Cancer · Bispecifics

J&J’s Two-Bispecific Combo Cuts Multiple Myeloma Progression Risk by 89% — an ‘Off-the-Rack’ Alternative to CAR-T Arrives

Fierce Pharma · Johnson & Johnson (NYSE: JNJ) | 2026.07.23

Key Facts

Johnson & Johnson released Phase 3 results combining two bispecific T-cell engagers, Tecvayli (targeting BCMA) and Talvey (targeting GPRC5D). In patients with relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma, the combo cut the risk of disease progression or death by 89% and overall risk of death by 62%. Unlike CAR-T, which needs per-patient manufacturing, both drugs are ‘off-the-shelf’ options that can be prescribed immediately, with no manufacturing wait. J&J plans to use this data to expand into earlier lines of treatment.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

CAR-T works extremely well, but it requires collecting the patient’s cells and ‘custom manufacturing’ for weeks. The bispecific combo delivers CAR-T-level effect by going after two targets, BCMA and GPRC5D, while being a finished product mass-produced in a factory, with no manufacturing wait. The treatment mechanism got more sophisticated by hitting two targets at once, but the process of getting the drug to the patient actually got simpler.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

In Korea too, access to CAR-T (Kymriah, Carvykti) is limited by manufacturing waits and high cost. If an off-the-shelf bispecific combo comes to Korea, multiple myeloma patients could start treatment much faster, and it’s likely to be easier to negotiate national health insurance coverage than for CAR-T. For Korean antibody CMOs and biosimilar companies, building bispecific production capacity is emerging as a new opportunity.

Story 3 · HIV Prevention · Market Access

Merck Announces an Early Supply Strategy for Its Once-Monthly Oral HIV Prevention Pill — Opening the Market Before Approval

Fierce Pharma · Merck (NYSE: MRK) | 2026.07.24

Key Facts

Merck switched on an early global supply and distribution system for alimatravir, its once-monthly oral HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) candidate, before final Phase 3 data has even been released. The reasoning: replacing daily pills or long-acting injections with one pill a month would greatly improve adherence and could lower new infection rates worldwide. Merck chose to line up licensing and supply channels ahead of approval to enter high-income and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) early and at the same time, aiming to seize the high ground in an HIV prevention market worth billions.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

With existing PrEP, you have to take a pill every day or get an injection every few months, and in practice, missed doses were the biggest cause of failure. Alimatravir is a strategy to cut the dosing burden itself, to once a month. On top of that, Merck laid down its distribution network before approval — clearing the pipeline before the product is even finished — so it can hit the market the moment it’s approved.

🇰🇷 The Korea Angle

New HIV infections in Korea are rising slowly, but PrEP awareness and prescribing are still low. A once-monthly oral option would give the KDCA and infectious disease specialists a new tool for prevention strategy. Above all, Merck’s approach of ‘lining up supply before approval’ is a useful reference for how early Korean companies need to prepare licensing-out and supply partnerships when going global.

🔬 This Week’s Deep Dive

Deep Dive · Where to Put the Complexity

Triple agonists, bispecifics, a monthly pill —
winning biotechs know where to put the complexity

① Same strategy, different directions

This week’s three stories span obesity, blood cancer and infectious disease, but the skeleton of the strategy is the same. Lilly raised its receptor targets to three, adding biological complexity to maximise efficacy. J&J did the opposite, stripping out CAR-T’s ‘custom manufacturing’ complexity to simplify to off-the-shelf. Merck cut dosing from daily to once a month, but in return took on a new complexity: building distribution before approval. None of the three ‘eliminated’ complexity — they just chose where to put it.

③ Why this distinction matters — complexity doesn’t disappear, it moves

Understanding biotech innovation in just one direction — ‘getting more sophisticated’ or ‘getting simpler’ — is wrong. In reality, complexity doesn’t disappear, it just moves. Lilly added complexity on the efficacy (mechanism) side; J&J removed it from manufacturing and supply but added it to mechanism design; and Merck reduced complexity in dosing but took on more in business development and distribution. Choosing which axis of complexity to carry is the strategy.

④ Career takeaway — which axis of complexity will you handle?

✓ Skill to build

The ability to speak both languages at once: mechanism and commercial. You should be able to explain why a multi-target mechanism raises efficacy, and also understand why commercial strategies like off-the-shelf manufacturing, PBM listing and global supply chains are needed. Clinical Development and Medical Affairs handle the former, Market Access and Business Development the latter — but people who move between both are the rarest.

⚠ Trap

Oversimplified thinking like ‘more targets make a better drug’ or ‘simpler is always the better strategy.’ Sometimes, like Lilly, adding targets to raise efficacy is the answer; sometimes, like J&J, removing manufacturing steps to improve access is. There isn’t one right answer — it depends on ‘what you’re trying to improve’ — and chasing trends without this judgement leaves you lost in both job-hunting and investing.

💡 Editor’s Insight

If I sum up this week in one sentence: ‘Complexity isn’t removed, it’s moved.’ Lilly put its complexity in the mechanism, J&J in the combo design instead of manufacturing and supply, and Merck in distribution and licensing. All three made something simpler, but in exchange, something else always got more complex. When you read biotech news, ask ‘what did this company make harder so that something else got easier?’ and you’ll see a lot more. Let’s keep reading with that eye in the next issue, bioworkers. 🙏

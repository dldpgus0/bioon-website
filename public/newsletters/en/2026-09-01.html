🔤 Word of the Week

PD-1×VEGF Bispecific Antibody

The mechanism behind Akeso's ivonescimab (Story 3)

Last issue's AstraZeneca story was about a bispecific that released two brakes of the same kind at once, PD-1 and CTLA-4, and the trial was stopped. Akeso's ivonescimab combines PD-1 (releasing an immune brake) with VEGF (blocking the blood vessels that feed a tumour): two completely different mechanisms in one antibody. Releasing two brakes is one strategy. Releasing a brake while cutting off the enemy's supply lines is a different one entirely.

💡 Analogy: Pulling another defender forward to strengthen your attack (AstraZeneca, doubling up on the same position) is one tactic. Keeping your attack the same but also blocking the goalkeeper's view (Akeso, hitting a completely different front at the same time) is another. Same sport, very different game plan.

Dual checkpoint blockade — AstraZeneca's volrustomig (stopped)

Blocks PD-1 and CTLA-4, two checkpoints from the same family. It looked stronger on paper, but extra side-effect and dosing variables meant it never beat Keytruda alone.

Checkpoint + anti-angiogenesis — Akeso's ivonescimab (succeeded)

Combines PD-1 blockade with VEGF blockade, two complementary mechanisms. It frees immune cells to attack while starving the tumour's blood supply, and beat Keytruda on survival.

📰 This Week's Top 3 Stories

STORY 1 · OBESITY DRUGS · LICENSING

Beyond Fat Loss: Roche Signs Up to $2.5B Deal for Hanmi's Muscle-Sparing Obesity Drug

Fierce Biotech · Roche (SIX: ROG) × Hanmi Pharmaceutical (KRX: 128940) | Aug 24, 2026

Key Facts

Roche secured global rights to Hanmi Pharmaceutical's clinical-stage UCN2 (urocortin 2) analogue for $190M upfront and up to $2.5B in total. GLP-1 blockbusters like Wegovy and Zepbound come with a well-known downside: patients lose a significant amount of muscle along with fat. Hanmi's candidate targets a different metabolic receptor from GLP-1, designed to burn fat while sparing muscle. It's a sign that Big Pharma is already looking beyond first-generation obesity drugs toward the quality of weight loss as the next battleground.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Muscle loss is a big reason metabolism slows and weight comes back after dieting. Hanmi's candidate leaves the GLP-1 pathway alone and flips a different metabolic switch that targets fat. The goal is still "lose weight," but what comes off is becoming the next thing companies compete on.

🌍 Global Lens

A Swiss giant licensing from a Korean developer shows Korean biotech becoming a real supplier of global obesity assets. Muscle loss also matters more in ageing societies such as Japan, Korea and much of Europe, where keeping older people strong is a public-health priority. The next obesity battleground may be what these drugs do for older patients.

Why it matters for your career: Seeing how a Korean clinical-stage asset gets valued and handed to a global pharma is a great case study in upfront-versus-milestone economics, the core vocabulary of BD.

STORY 2 · GENE THERAPY · REGULATORY RISK

Spine Scan Surprise: FDA Halts Regenxbio's Gene Therapy Refiling Over Safety Concerns

Fierce Biotech · Regenxbio (NASDAQ: RGNX) | Aug 24, 2026

Key Facts

Regenxbio's plan to refile RGX-121, its gene therapy for Hunter syndrome, was derailed after the FDA placed a clinical hold on the programme over unexpected MRI findings in the spine. One-time genetic treatments promise to fix devastating childhood disorders for good, but delivering viral vectors close to the central nervous system carries serious, unpredictable safety risk.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Gene therapy is appealing because it rewrites DNA to fix a disease at its source. But as soon as you inject the viral vector that carries it near the brain or spine, the chance of an unexpected reaction rises. The cause of this MRI finding isn't confirmed yet, but the pattern holds: the bigger the promise of a permanent fix, the higher the safety bar the delivery method has to clear.

🌍 Global Lens

Hunter syndrome is a global story. In Japan, JCR Pharmaceuticals' enzyme therapy designed to cross the blood-brain barrier was approved in 2021, so some patients already have a brain-targeted option. Any gene therapy has to clear that bar, and a US safety hold can slow plans elsewhere, because other regulators look closely at the FDA's reasons.

Why it matters for your career: Understanding why a hold happens and what it takes to lift one is directly useful for regulatory and CMC roles in gene therapy, where these moments pull those teams to the centre.

📌 One unexpected spine MRI finding stopped an entire refiling — the hidden risk behind gene therapy's biggest promise. — Fierce Biotech summary

STORY 3 · IMMUNO-ONCOLOGY · HEAD-TO-HEAD

The Keytruda Challenger: Akeso Reports a Survival Win for Its Dual-Action Antibody in Lung Cancer

BioSpace · Akeso (HKEX: 9926) | Aug 28, 2026

Key Facts

Akeso's PD-1×VEGF bispecific antibody ivonescimab achieved a statistically significant improvement in overall survival (OS) over standard chemotherapy and existing immunotherapies at a Phase 3 interim analysis in advanced lung cancer. It's being described as one of the very few therapies to beat Keytruda (pembrolizumab) head-to-head, and it came just weeks after AstraZeneca's dual-checkpoint bispecific failed to do the same.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Most immunotherapies do one job: release an immune brake. Ivonescimab does that while also blocking the blood vessels that feed the tumour, a Swiss Army knife of an antibody. Last issue, AstraZeneca added a second brake of the same kind and failed. Akeso added a completely different capability, cutting off blood supply, and it worked.

🌍 Global Lens

Akeso is Chinese and ivonescimab was approved in China first, which makes it one of the clearest cases of a China-born drug driving the global standard-of-care debate. Approval in the West depends on whether data from mostly Chinese patients will satisfy the FDA and EMA, which is why Summit's global trials matter so much.

Why it matters for your career: Clinical development and combination strategy teams look for exactly this kind of analytical thinking: not "bispecifics are exciting," but why this one worked.

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · The Shadow of the First Generation

① Same question, different endings

All three stories are about the weak spots of first-generation blockbusters. Roche and Hanmi patched GLP-1's weak spot, muscle loss, with a different pathway. Akeso vaulted past the ceiling of PD-1 monotherapy. Regenxbio tripped over the hidden risk behind gene therapy's biggest promise, a permanent one-dose cure. Last issue I said taking on a giant head-on means losing. Akeso broke that rule, but not by colliding head-on: it came from a different angle, checkpoint blockade plus anti-angiogenesis.

③ Why it matters — you clear a wall from a new angle, not with a harder push

Akeso cleared the wall I said last week nobody could. The trick wasn't one more of the same weapon; it was one more, completely different weapon. AstraZeneca failed by stacking two checkpoint inhibitors; Akeso succeeded with a complementary combination. Beating a giant doesn't take pushing harder in the same direction. It takes a different angle.

④ Career takeaway — redundant or complementary?

✓ Skill to build

When two mechanisms are combined in one drug, learn to ask whether the combination is redundant or complementary. That single question often decides success or failure in combination drug development, and being able to answer it quickly is core to translational science and combination strategy roles.

⚠ Trap

Assuming that adding another mechanism is automatically good. Put last issue's AstraZeneca case (redundant checkpoints, failed) next to this week's Akeso case (complementary mechanisms, succeeded) and it's clear the real question isn't how many mechanisms you add, but which. Memorise "bispecific = innovation" without that distinction and you'll trip up in interviews and investment calls alike.

💡 Editor's Insight

If there's one line for this week, it's this: you clear a wall from a different angle, not with a harder push. I'll admit it was a little awkward to write "stand against a giant and you lose" and see a counterexample a week later. But looking closer, it's the same lesson: Akeso didn't fight the giant the giant's way either. See you next issue. 🙏

🔤 Word of the Week

International reference pricing

a.k.a. most-favoured-nation (MFN) pricing, the idea behind CMS's GLOBE model (Story 2)

Setting a drug's price by asking "what do other countries pay for this?" is called international reference pricing. Many European countries, and Korea, already do some version of it. The US has been the outlier: manufacturers set the price and insurers or the government negotiate afterwards, which made it the most expensive market in the world. Now CMS has said that for Part B drugs, the ones administered by a doctor or in a hospital, it will use prices from economically comparable countries as the cost benchmark.

💡 Analogy: It's like a shopper who knows the same item is much cheaper on an overseas site and asks, "why is it so much more here?" Now the shopper asking is the US government, and the shop being asked is the drug company.

Until now — "we set our own price" 🇺🇸

Manufacturers set the US price and payers negotiated afterwards. What other countries paid had no direct effect on what the US paid.

GLOBE — "what do other countries pay?" 🌍

For Part B drugs, comparison countries' prices become the cost benchmark. A low price abroad can pull the US benchmark down, so where and when you launch starts to affect US revenue.

STORY 1 · CHINA SOURCING · AUTOIMMUNE

Novartis Bets Up to $7.8B on Abogen's mRNA "In-Body" T-Cell Engager for Autoimmune Disease

Fierce Biotech · Novartis (NYSE: NVS) × Abogen Biosciences | Oct 2, 2026

Key Facts

Novartis is paying China-based Abogen Biosciences $575 million upfront and up to $7.2 billion in milestones for global rights to ABO2203. The drug is an mRNA-encoded CD19xCD3 T-cell engager delivered in lipid nanoparticles, so the patient's own body makes the engager after injection. Its lead indication is immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), and it is also being tested in B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The deal includes an option on Abogen's next-generation RNA platform, so Novartis is buying a way into a technology as well as one asset.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

CAR-T means taking a patient's cells out, engineering them and putting them back, with lymphodepleting chemotherapy and a hospital stay first. ABO2203 aims to deplete B cells with an injection, which could be given in an outpatient setting and possibly at lower cost. The timing stands out: Novartis recently paused an autoimmune CAR-T programme after three deaths (we covered it in #15), so this reads as a move to a lower-risk way into the same market. It's still early-stage, though, so efficacy and safety have yet to be proven.

🌍 Global Lens

China is again the source of a next-generation modality, the second big China deal in two issues after Lilly and InnoCare. Other hubs such as Korea, Japan and Europe have to decide whether to compete for the same early-stage assets or on other strengths like manufacturing and clinical execution. Regulators including the FDA will watch the early safety data closely, because an engager produced inside the body is a new kind of product.

Why it matters for your career: BD teams need people who can value an option on a platform, not just a single asset, and scientists who understand mRNA-LNP delivery and T-cell engagers will be in demand.

STORY 2 · MARKET ACCESS · US PRICING POLICY

CMS Locks In "GLOBE," Bringing Most-Favored-Nation Pricing to Medicare Part B

Fierce Healthcare · CMS | Sep 30, 2026

Key Facts

CMS finalised the Global Benchmark for Efficient Drug Pricing (GLOBE) Model, which sets cost benchmarks for physician-administered Part B drugs using prices from economically comparable countries. It runs from January 1, 2027 to March 31, 2032 and covers about 25% of traditional Medicare beneficiaries in randomly selected regions. Patients there should see lower out-of-pocket coinsurance from April 1, 2027. After public comments, CMS excluded biosimilars, reference biologics once a biosimilar launches, orphan drugs, plasma-derived products and some cell and gene therapies.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

In plain terms, the US now formally prices against international benchmarks. If a company launches in a lower-priced market like the UK or Korea at a low price, that price can feed back into the US benchmark. So launch sequencing and list-price corridors stop being an ex-US sales question and become a US revenue decision. The exemptions also give orphan, biosimilar and advanced-therapy portfolios a clear advantage within Part B.

🌍 Global Lens

GLOBE is a US model, but it reaches every market it references. Prices agreed with payers such as NICE in England or in Korea's HIRA-linked system can now affect US benchmarks, which may delay or reshape launches in lower-priced markets like the UK and Korea. Companies will have to plan country by country, with Washington in the room.

Why it matters for your career: Global pricing, market access and launch-strategy teams will need people who can model how a foreign price flows into US revenue, and who can connect payer policy to commercial strategy.

📌 Foreign prices now shape the US benchmark, so launch order has become revenue strategy. — Fierce Healthcare summary

STORY 3 · COMMERCIAL STRATEGY · KOREAN BIOSIMILARS

Teva and Samsung Bioepis Expand Their Alliance with Six New Biosimilars

Fierce Pharma · Teva (NYSE: TEVA) × Samsung Bioepis | Oct 2, 2026

Key Facts

Teva and Korea's Samsung Bioepis agreed to co-develop and commercialise up to six biosimilar candidates; financial terms were not disclosed. Two candidates reference AstraZeneca's Fasenra and one references Novartis's Ilaris. Samsung Bioepis will handle development, regulatory work and manufacturing, while Teva commercialises in the US, Europe and Canada, with an option to add other territories. It is the third deal between the companies in 2026, after Epysqli (a Soliris biosimilar) in the US in January and Opuviz (an Eylea biosimilar) in Canada this summer.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

As major biologics lose patent protection from the late 2020s into the early 2030s, more biosimilar competition should lower prices for severe asthma and rare autoinflammatory diseases and widen access to them. What's interesting is that the split of roles is hardening: a Korean company develops and manufactures, a Western partner sells. It also fits Teva CEO Richard Francis's "Pivot to Growth" strategy, a big US commercial platform that partners for development rather than funding R&D itself.

🌍 Global Lens

Samsung Bioepis is the developer and manufacturer for markets reached through Teva's commercial network in the US, Europe and Canada. As patents on major biologics expire, payers in all those markets will be looking for cheaper biosimilars, and both the FDA and the EMA have established biosimilar pathways. For other Asian manufacturers, this is a template for moving up the value chain.

Why it matters for your career: Biosimilar programmes need analytical, CMC and regulatory people, plus alliance managers who can run a partnership where one company builds and another sells.

📌 Korea builds, the West sells, and this year the pattern has now been set three times. — Fierce Pharma summary

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · The Value Chain Is Being Redrawn

Sourced in China, built in Korea, priced against a US benchmark —
this week's news was a map of the biotech division of labour

① Three stories fold into one map

Read separately, these are a licensing deal, a policy rule and a partnership. Laid on top of each other, they show where each country stands. China is sourcing new modalities, even early-stage ones, at scale. Korea is selling development and manufacturing capability to Western commercial networks. The US is using the size of its market to set price benchmarks. Companies like Novartis and Teva read that map and decide where to buy, where to build and where to sell.

China · discovery US · price benchmark Korea · development & manufacturing

③ Why it matters — the same news reads differently from each seat

A big licensing deal is an opportunity for a Chinese biotech, but a Korean biotech has to ask what it will be chosen for. Once US price benchmarks bite, launches in lower-priced markets can slip down the queue. The same headline can be an opportunity or a risk depending on where you sit, which is why knowing where your company and role sit on this map turns news into something that is about your own work.

④ Career takeaway — knowing which seat you're in

✓ Skill to build

If you're drawn to BD and alliances, look at who buys from whom and who shares the selling. If you're in pricing and market access, study how foreign price links change launch strategy. If you're in CMC or regulatory, biosimilar and new-modality development skills are the ones to build. In every case, you should be able to explain where your target company sits on the value chain; that's what separates interview answers.

⚠ Trap

Believing "Korea is a development and manufacturing hub" means manufacturing alone is enough. Commercial and pricing control sit with the partner, and a change in US benchmarks can shake launch strategy too. Manufacturing skill plus a reading of market structure is what shows you where the room to grow is.

💡 Editor's Insight

If I had to put this week in one line: the biotech value chain is dividing up by geography, and every role sits somewhere on it. Korea has a good seat today; whether it stays at manufacturing or climbs another rung is still open. Next time you read a deal headline, ask "where on the chain does this company actually stand?"

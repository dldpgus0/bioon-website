BIO:ON Insight EN #23 · Nov 3, 2026 · Korean Fabry Out-Licence · Lilly's Q3 · Ferring Buys Gameto

Fewer injections, more value?
Three price tags on easier treatment

A $475M Korean out-licence for a subcutaneous Fabry therapy, Lilly's $24.4B quarter and Ferring's deal for Gameto. This week's three stories, read through one idea: treatment burden.

Last issue was about where companies find their next growth engine. This week I kept noticing the fuel: the burden patients carry just to get treated. Money moved towards turning a fortnightly infusion into a subcutaneous shot, and towards cutting two weeks of hormone injections down to a few days. Yet Lilly's quarter shows that easier doesn't automatically mean better-selling. So how does the industry put a price on convenience?
— Editor · MSc Biotechnology & Pharmaceutical Management

🔤 Word of the Week

Treatment burden

a.k.a. the time and effort a patient spends just getting the medicine

Treatment burden is everything a patient has to put up with to receive a therapy, separate from how well it works: hours in an infusion chair, travel to clinic, pain, cost, how many doses a day. It matters most when treatment goes on for years, as with lifelong enzyme therapy for a rare disease or repeated IVF cycles. That is why drugmakers treat longer dosing intervals and new routes of administration as serious ways to stand out.

💡 Analogy: If a drug's efficacy is the destination, treatment burden is the journey. A direct train beats three changes of bus. But if the direct train drops you well short of where you need to go, people will go back to changing buses. Convenience wins when efficacy is close.

Lower the burden — "fewer shots, fewer trips" 🪶

Stretch the dosing interval, swap an infusion for a subcutaneous shot or a pill, shorten the course. LA-GLA and Fertilo sit on this side.

Raise the efficacy — "work better" 🎯

Focus on a bigger effect in the same disease. This quarter, Lilly's growth came from the injectables Mounjaro and Zepbound, not its new pill.

📰 This Week's Top 3 Stories

STORY 1 · OUT-LICENSING · RARE DISEASE

From Fortnightly Infusions to a Subcutaneous Shot? GC Biopharma and Hanmi License a Fabry Therapy to BioMarin for Up to $475M

BioMarin press release · Korea Herald | Oct 29, 2026

Key Facts

BioMarin (Nasdaq: BMRN) has taken an exclusive worldwide licence, outside South Korea, to HM15421/GC1134A (LA-GLA), a long-acting enzyme replacement therapy for Fabry disease co-developed by GC Biopharma and Hanmi Pharm. The Korean partners get $70M upfront, up to $405M in development and commercial milestones and tiered royalties on net sales. After the ongoing phase 1/2 study, BioMarin takes over later development, manufacturing and commercialisation outside Korea, and GC and Hanmi keep the Korean rights. The FDA granted orphan drug designation in 2024.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Fabry disease is an X-linked lysosomal storage disorder: changes in the GLA gene leave patients short of the enzyme alpha-galactosidase A, so a fatty substance builds up and drives pain, kidney failure and higher heart attack and stroke risk. Enzyme replacement is the standard of care, and conventional ERTs are usually given by intravenous infusion every two weeks. LA-GLA pairs Hanmi's long-acting platform with GC's enzyme expertise (it makes Hunterase for Hunter syndrome) to give a longer half-life and a subcutaneous shot. BioMarin says it could become the first subcutaneous ERT for Fabry. It already sells the oral drug Galafold for patients with amenable mutations, and it bought Amicus earlier this year, so this would give it a second Fabry option for patients Galafold can't reach.

🌍 Global Lens

Two Korean companies combined their strengths, took the asset into the clinic themselves and then handed it to a global rare-disease specialist while keeping their home market. Of the $475M headline, only the $70M upfront is fixed, and how GC and Hanmi split the money has not been disclosed.

Why it matters for your career: BD and licensing teams negotiate deals like this, and regulatory and country-level commercial teams turn retained home-market rights into an actual launch.

📌 "This agreement reflects our growing commitment to the Fabry community." — Alexander Hardy, BioMarin CEO

STORY 2 · EARNINGS · COMMERCIAL STRATEGY

The Shots Outsold the Pill: Lilly Posts $24.4B in Q3 and Raises 2026 Guidance to $91–92B

CNBC · Eli Lilly (NYSE: LLY) | Oct 29, 2026

Key Facts

Eli Lilly reported third-quarter revenue of $24.4B, up 39% and ahead of the $22.61B analysts expected. Mounjaro rose 65% to $10.72B and Zepbound 49% to $5.35B. Foundayo (orforglipron), Lilly's once-daily obesity pill, brought in $184M, short of the roughly $229M expected. Lilly raised its full-year revenue guidance from $85–87B to $91–92B, and the shares rose about 7% on the day.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

The interesting part sits under the headline. In the US, volume grew 41% while realised prices fell 7%; outside the US, volume rose 76% and prices fell 29%. Lower prices after Lilly's 2025 pricing deal with the US government, plus a Medicare pilot covering weight-loss GLP-1s since July, came with far more patients treated. And although a pill looks like the easier option, the growth came from the injectables. Foundayo's US prescriptions and new patients did double on the previous quarter, but to me this reads as a sign that proven injectables still set a high bar. About $900M of the quarter was one-off, including a $410M Jardiance milestone.

🌍 Global Lens

As prices fall and volumes climb, the obesity market rewards clear differentiation over another me-too incretin. Hanmi's HM17321, licensed to Genentech in August for up to $2.3B ($190M upfront), targets the CRF2 receptor and aims to cut fat while preserving muscle, a different lane from semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Why it matters for your career: Finance and FP&A teams split growth into price and volume, and market access teams design the coverage deals, like Medicare's pilot, that drive that volume.

📌 "room for expansion here of this market is dramatic" — Dave Ricks, Lilly CEO, to CNBC

STORY 3 · M&A · WOMEN'S HEALTH

Two Weeks of Hormone Shots Down to Days? Ferring Agrees to Buy IVF Start-up Gameto for Up to $1.38B

BioPharma Dive · pharmaphorum · Ferring × Gameto | Oct 27, 2026

Key Facts

Privately held Swiss drugmaker Ferring has agreed to acquire Gameto, an Austin, Texas cell-engineering company, for up to $1.3825B. The upfront was not disclosed; most of the value depends on development, regulatory and commercial milestones. The main asset is Fertilo, which uses stem cell-derived ovarian support cells to mature eggs outside the body. It is cleared in Australia and parts of Latin America and is in phase 3 in the US. Founded in 2020, Gameto has raised $127M. The companies will run separately until the deal closes.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Standard IVF usually means about two weeks of daily hormone injections to grow as many mature eggs as possible, and it is costly and often repeated. Gameto says Fertilo could remove the need for those injections and shorten an IVF or egg-freezing cycle to as little as three days. The twist: Ferring sells those very hormones (Menopur, Rekovelle), and Menopur was one of the main drivers of its 2025 revenue of more than €2.5B, up 10%. Ferring is buying a technology that could shrink demand for its own best-seller. Managers call that cannibalisation, and I read it as a choice to disrupt itself before a rival does.

🌍 Global Lens

Fertilo already has clearances in Australia and parts of Latin America, so FDA review after the US phase 3 is the big next test. IVF is often paid for out of pocket or through national schemes that vary widely by country, so shorter, cheaper cycles could matter to payers as well as patients; the sources give no pricing or reimbursement plans.

Why it matters for your career: Corporate development and M&A teams value targets and design milestone-based consideration, and portfolio strategy teams manage the clash between an existing franchise and the technology that might replace it.

📌 "a defining moment for Gameto" — Dina Turner, Gameto CEO and co-founder

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · Pricing Convenience

A subcutaneous shot, a daily pill, a three-day IVF cycle.
Three price tags on lower treatment burden

① Treatment burden has a price tag

Follow the money this week and one pattern shows up. BioMarin paid $70M upfront for "infusion to subcutaneous", and Ferring put up to $1.38B on "two weeks of hormone shots to a few days". Yet the cash this quarter poured into Lilly's injectables, while its once-daily pill came in below forecasts. Convenience is worth money, but it doesn't stand in for an efficacy gap. So buyers attach conditions instead of paying for convenience up front.

③ Why it matters: convenience is conditional value

In medicines, convenience decides the contest when efficacy is similar. When efficacy is weaker, convenience alone rarely changes prescribing. That is why, in both of this week's deals, the guaranteed money is small or undisclosed and the rest only arrives after trials and approvals. The value of "easier" is held back until data prove it.

④ Career takeaway: the people who turn patient experience into numbers

✓ Skill to build

Across all three stories the same skill keeps appearing: translating a better patient experience into a number a company can act on. In deal teams that means deciding how much of a convenience upgrade to pay up front and how much to tie to milestones. In HEOR and market access it means showing what fewer infusions and clinic visits are worth to a payer. In finance it means separating price from volume. If you can do this for one real product in an interview, you stand out.

⚠ Trap

Calling the winner from convenience language alone: "first subcutaneous", "one pill a day", "three days". LA-GLA is still in phase 1/2, Fertilo is still in US phase 3, and Foundayo, the easy pill, missed forecasts this quarter. Convenience only earns a price when efficacy and safety hold up.

💡 Editor's Insight

After reading all three, I came away thinking that "how hard is it to receive this drug?" is as serious a question as "how well does it work?". But Lilly's quarter is a reminder that the answer still shows up in trial data and sales. Next time you see "first subcutaneous" or "one pill a day" in a headline, ask how much efficacy came along with it. See you next issue. 🙏

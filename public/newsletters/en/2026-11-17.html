🔤 Word of the Week

R&D financing

a.k.a. keeping the rights, outsourcing the development bill

R&D financing is when an outside investor, often a private equity or life sciences fund, pays for part of a specific drug's development and gets paid back (through milestones or royalties) only if the drug is approved or hits sales targets. If the drug fails, the investor loses its money. The drugmaker keeps the rights while shifting some of the cost and failure risk of expensive phase 3 trials; the investor gets a direct bet on one drug rather than on a whole company. Blackstone's up-to-$1.5B deal for Pfizer's obesity drug is this week's example.

💡 Analogy: Think of a shop owner who wants to expand. Instead of taking on a business partner, they take money from an investor for the building work and promise a share of takings if the new space does well. The owner still owns the shop, but has to share the upside, and the investor shares the loss if it flops.

Out-licensing — "hand over the rights, get paid" 🤝

The partner takes development and commercial rights and pays upfront and milestones. If the drug succeeds, most of the sales belong to the partner.

R&D financing — "keep the rights, borrow the budget" 💰

You still develop and sell the drug yourself, but you owe the investor its agreed share if it succeeds.

📰 This Week's Top 3 Stories

STORY 1 · APPROVAL · PORTFOLIO STRATEGY

Failed in AF, Approved in Stroke: Bayer's Sunharbo Becomes the First FDA-Approved Factor XIa Inhibitor

BioPharma Dive · TCTMD · pharmaphorum · Bayer | Nov 9, 2026

Key Facts

The FDA approved Bayer's Sunharbo (asundexian) on 9 November, the first oral factor XIa inhibitor cleared in the US. It is for adults with a recent non-cardioembolic ischaemic stroke or high-risk TIA who are taking antiplatelet therapy, at 50 mg once daily. In the phase 3 OCEANIC-STROKE trial of more than 12,000 people, it cut recurrent ischaemic stroke by a relative 26% (2.2 percentage points absolute over two years) with no rise in major bleeding, though GI bleeding was a little higher (2.7 vs 2.1 per 100 patient-years). China approved it in October, and the US list price is $748.50 for 30 days.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Factor XIa inhibitors were developed to prevent clots with less bleeding than today's anticoagulants. Sunharbo, though, failed its phase 3 trial in atrial fibrillation, the biggest prize, and AF is not on the label. So Bayer came in through a different door: preventing a second stroke. The commercial stakes are clear. Xarelto sales fell almost 43% in the first half of 2026 (€738M) under generic competition. Analysts suggest Sunharbo could reach about €3B a year, while Bayer's own forecast was trimmed to about €1B after the AF failure. BMS and J&J's milvexian has had several setbacks, and Novartis is developing the injectable antibody abelacimab.

🌍 Global Lens

Europe and Japan are reviewing the drug, and China has already approved it. Payers everywhere will weigh a branded first-in-class pill against cheap generic antiplatelets and newly generic anticoagulants; one US stroke neurologist told TCTMD that cost is probably the main limiting factor.

Why it matters for your career: Medical affairs teams explain a new class to prescribers, market access teams build the value case payers need, and product strategy teams re-plan a portfolio after a failed lead indication.

📌 "a bold step forwards in post-stroke care" — Christian Rommel, head of R&D, Bayer

STORY 2 · R&D FINANCING · OBESITY

Private Equity Pays for the Phase 3s: Blackstone Puts Up to $1.5B into Pfizer's Monthly Obesity Shot

BioPharma Dive · Pfizer (NYSE: PFE) × Blackstone | Nov 11, 2026

Key Facts

Blackstone Life Sciences will put up to $1.5B into Pfizer's development of berobenatide, a potentially once-monthly GLP-1 injection that Pfizer bought with Metsera for about $10B after a bidding war with Novo Nordisk. The money supports as many as 10 ongoing or planned pivotal trials and will be phased in through 2028. In return, Pfizer pays Blackstone milestones, spread over several years, if the FDA approves berobenatide as a once-monthly single-agent weight-loss drug and if it reaches certain sales goals.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Why would a company Pfizer's size take outside money? Obesity phase 3s enrol thousands of patients and may need head-to-head trials against Zepbound and Wegovy, while Pfizer faces generic competition for Eliquis and Ibrance. Its CFO said the deal lets Pfizer push its obesity programme and keep the financial flexibility to invest in the rest of its pipeline. Pfizer keeps the rights and shares part of the cost and failure risk. Blackstone has done similar deals with Alnylam (up to $2B), Moderna ($750M) and Merck ($700M).

🌍 Global Lens

Smaller biotechs in South Korea and elsewhere usually fund late-stage work by out-licensing and handing over rights. Large-scale R&D financing offers a route that keeps the rights at home, and how far it spreads beyond big pharma is worth watching.

Why it matters for your career: Corporate development and finance teams structure these deals, valuation analysts model a single drug's probability of success and sales, and healthcare investment funds are a growing employer for science graduates.

📌 "a good example of our leadership in providing capital solutions at scale" — Nicholas Galakatos, Blackstone Life Sciences

STORY 3 · M&A · PLATFORM

From Antibodies to Pills? argenx Agrees to Buy Oral Peptide Specialist Unnatural Products for Up to $1.15B

argenx press release · pharmaphorum · argenx (NASDAQ: ARGX) | Nov 9, 2026

Key Facts

argenx will acquire Unnatural Products (UNP) of Santa Cruz, California, for $550M upfront from cash on hand plus up to $595M on specified development and regulatory milestones, about $1.15B in total. UNP's platform combines parallel experimentation and machine learning to discover oral macrocyclic peptides. argenx already held a minority stake and signed a research collaboration and option deal worth up to $1.5B with UNP in July 2025. The deal is expected to close by the end of 2026.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

argenx built its business on Vyvgart, an FcRn-blocking antibody for myasthenia gravis and CIDP. Recently a Sjögren's trial failed and the FDA asked for another trial in dermatomyositis, so it needs growth beyond one franchise. Antibodies are injected, but macrocyclic peptides can be made as pills; the 2025 collaboration was aimed at a once-daily oral FcRn inhibitor. argenx worked with UNP for more than two years before buying it, then split the price roughly in half between cash now and milestones later.

🌍 Global Lens

If FcRn blockade moves from injections to pills, the competitive map changes for every FcRn developer, from big pharma to partnered assets out of South Korea and China. UNP has also worked with MSD, Novartis and BridgeBio, which shows how widely pharma is looking at oral peptides.

Why it matters for your career: BD and alliance managers test a target through a collaboration before a buyout, M&A teams value a discovery platform, and integration teams merge research groups after the deal closes.

📌 "highly complementary to our antibody engineering" — Karen Massey, CEO, argenx

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · Who Carries the Risk

Go it alone, borrow or buy.
Three ways to share risk at the edge of a patent cliff

① Same homework, different answers

All three companies face the same problem: a revenue engine slowing under generics or setbacks, and a need for the next one. The difference is who carries the development risk. Bayer took the AF failure on its own balance sheet and still won an approval in stroke. Pfizer kept the rights but split phase 3 costs with Blackstone. argenx bought a company it had already tested for two years, and deferred about half the price to milestones. The less risk you carry, the more you share when it works.

③ Why it matters: a deal structure reveals a strategy

None of these is the right answer in general; each fits a company's cash and conviction. With conviction and cash, you carry the risk and keep the upside. With conviction but too many priorities, you bring in outside capital. With an attractive but unproven technology, you collaborate first and then buy in instalments. A deal's structure tells you how much a company believes in its own drug.

④ Career takeaway: reading risk and money together

✓ Skill to build

The skill all three stories reward is explaining one drug's odds of success alongside the company's financial position. In corporate development and finance, that means judging whether licensing, financing or acquisition fits a company. In BD, it means using upfront and milestones to move risk between parties. In product strategy and market access, it means recalculating what a market is worth after a lead indication fails. Bring one worked example to an interview and you stand out.

⚠ Trap

Reading the future from headline numbers: "$1.5B investment", "$1.15B acquisition", "first approval". Blackstone's money has to be paid back if Pfizer succeeds, about half of argenx's price is still conditional, and Sunharbo missed atrial fibrillation, the biggest market. Read who took on which risk, and on what terms.

💡 Editor's Insight

After these three stories I came away thinking drug development is as much about how risk is shared as about the science. Facing the same patent cliff, one company went it alone, one borrowed and one bought. Next time you read a deal story, ask who loses money if it fails. See you next issue. 🙏

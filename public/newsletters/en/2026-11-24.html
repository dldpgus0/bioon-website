What does the FDA need
before it says yes?

Summit and Akeso's rejection, Roche's early approval for Wilserdly and Regeneron's RNAi debut with Livsayis. This week's three stories, read through one question: what evidence wins an approval.

Last issue was about who carries the risk of developing a drug. This week showed where that risk ends: the FDA's decision. Three verdicts landed within days. Ivonescimab, once called a "Keytruda killer", slowed cancer growth but could not show patients lived longer, and was turned down. Roche's Wilserdly showed a large effect in patients with one gene mutation and was approved a month early. Regeneron's Livsayis entered a crowded market on the strength of four doses a year. An approval is not a verdict that a drug works; it is a verdict that the evidence is enough.
— Editor · MSc Biotechnology & Pharmaceutical Management

🔤 Word of the Week

PFS vs OS

a.k.a. the two report cards of a cancer trial

Progression-free survival (PFS) is how long patients live without their cancer growing or spreading. Overall survival (OS) is simply how long patients live, from any cause. PFS reads out sooner and needs fewer patients, so trials use it a lot, but holding a tumour back does not always mean living longer. When a drug has real side effects or the evidence is borderline, the FDA wants to see OS. This week ivonescimab had a strong PFS hazard ratio of 0.52, but the OS difference was not statistically significant, and that sank the application.

💡 Analogy: In a marathon, PFS is your split at halfway and OS is whether you cross the finish line. A fast first half suggests a good finish, but a runner who goes out too hard can fade. If the referee (the FDA) insists on the finishing time, a great halfway split will not win a medal.

PFS — "time the cancer was held back" ⏸️

Faster and cheaper to measure, but it may not answer the question patients care about most: will I live longer?

OS — "time the patient lived" ❤️

The most convincing report card, but it takes longer, and later treatments can blur the result.

📰 This Week's Top 3 Stories

STORY 1 · REJECTION · ONCOLOGY

Strong PFS, So Why Not? FDA Rejects Summit and Akeso's Ivonescimab in Lung Cancer

BioSpace · pharmaphorum · Healio · BioPharma Dive · Summit (NASDAQ: SMMT) | Nov 15, 2026

Key Facts

On Sunday 15 November Summit Therapeutics said the FDA had issued a complete response letter for ivonescimab, a PD-1 × VEGF bispecific antibody, given with chemotherapy in EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer after an EGFR inhibitor. The filing rested on the phase 3 HARMONi trial of 438 patients. Progression-free survival was 6.8 vs 4.4 months (hazard ratio 0.52), but overall survival, 16.8 vs 14 months (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.62–1.01), was not statistically significant. The FDA said substantial evidence of effectiveness is not established without a significant OS benefit.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Akeso developed ivonescimab and Summit licensed it in 2022 in a deal worth up to $5B, including $500M upfront. It is already approved for three lung cancer uses in China, where it beat Keytruda head to head. The sticking point was who was in the trial: 70% of HARMONi patients (306) were Asian, and the OS hazard ratio in the Western subgroup moved between 0.98, 0.84 and 0.76 depending on when it was analysed. Summit argued OS was moving towards significance; the FDA was not persuaded. Summit shares fell 6% on the Friday before the decision and another 15% on Monday, and Akeso dropped 22% in Hong Kong (BioPharma Dive).

🌍 Global Lens

This is a lesson for any company hoping to win a US approval with data drawn mostly from one region, including developers in South Korea and China who run large multinational trials. PD-1 × VEGF bispecifics are also being developed by BMS with BioNTech, Pfizer, AbbVie and MSD, so the bar for evidence will shape the whole class.

Why it matters for your career: Clinical development teams design multinational trials, biostatisticians write the analysis plans that decide what counts as a win, and regulatory affairs teams agree the evidence standard with the FDA before a trial starts.

📌 "We are disappointed for patients and their families" — Robert Duggan, co-CEO, Summit Therapeutics

STORY 2 · APPROVAL · BREAST CANCER

A Month Ahead of Schedule: FDA Approves Roche's Oral Breast Cancer Pill Wilserdly, Cutting Progression Risk by 62%

Roche press release · BioPharma Dive · Roche (SIX: ROG) | Nov 16, 2026

Key Facts

The FDA approved Roche's Wilserdly (giredestrant) on 16 November, about a month before its 18 December deadline. It is for adults with ER-positive, HER2-negative, ESR1-mutated advanced or metastatic breast cancer whose disease came back during or after adjuvant hormone therapy, or progressed after hormone therapy for metastatic disease. It is taken with everolimus. In the phase 3 evERA trial it cut the risk of progression or death by 62% in ESR1-mutated patients (HR 0.38, p<0.0001). The US list price is $22,500 a month.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

Wilserdly is an oral SERD, a drug that breaks down the oestrogen receptor. Lilly and AstraZeneca already sell oral SERDs, and all three are cleared for ESR1-mutated patients. The fast approval came from picking the right patients: across everyone in the trial the risk reduction was 44%, but in the mutated group it rose to 62%. The bigger prize comes next. Roche has filed for use after surgery to prevent relapse (the lidERA trial), with an FDA decision due by 30 November. Wilserdly is the only oral SERD to show benefit in that adjuvant setting, and RBC has estimated peak sales of up to $14B a year.

🌍 Global Lens

Wilserdly is used only after an FDA-authorised test confirms an ESR1 mutation. As more drugs depend on a companion test, approval and reimbursement for the drug and the test have to move together in every market, which also opens opportunities for diagnostics companies.

Why it matters for your career: Translational scientists use biomarkers to find the patients who benefit most, regulatory teams file the drug and its companion test together, and market access teams map the patient journey from test to prescription.

📌 "The key debate is not whether oral SERDs work, it's where they fit" — Michael Leuchten, analyst, Jefferies

STORY 3 · APPROVAL · RNAi

Four Shots a Year: FDA Approves Regeneron's First RNAi Drug, Livsayis, for Myasthenia Gravis

BioPharma Dive · Regeneron (NASDAQ: REGN) × Alnylam | Nov 18, 2026

Key Facts

The FDA approved Regeneron's Livsayis on 18 November for adults with AChR antibody-positive generalised myasthenia gravis. It is an RNA interference (RNAi) drug that stops the body making C5, a protein involved in the faulty immune attack, and is given as an injection under the skin four times a year. It is Regeneron's first RNAi drug and, according to BioPharma Dive, the first RNAi therapy approved for an autoimmune disease. Alnylam discovered it and licensed it to Regeneron in 2019, so Alnylam receives a $25M approval milestone plus royalties. A year's supply lists at $410,000.

Plain-English Breakdown 🧩

The myasthenia gravis market is already busy: argenx's popular drug (Vyvgart, covered in issue #25) and AstraZeneca medicines that block C5 in other ways. AstraZeneca's newly approved Klygefa is given once a week. Livsayis competes on dosing interval: RNAi silences the messenger RNA that carries the instructions for C5, so the effect lasts for months. TD Cowen cites best-in-class efficacy and convenience and sees $1.5B in peak sales, while RBC expects uptake to be measured at first against well-entrenched rivals. The drug was first aimed at another disease, PNH, but did not show enough on its own and changed direction.

🌍 Global Lens

As long-acting RNAi moves into autoimmune disease, every developer in the space, including FcRn antibody and RNA drug companies in South Korea and elsewhere, will be compared on dosing frequency as well as efficacy. Alnylam's model of licensing to a larger partner for milestones and royalties is also the usual path for platform biotechs.

Why it matters for your career: RNA drug design and delivery scientists build these medicines, market access teams set price and coverage for rare-disease drugs, and product strategy teams position a newcomer against established rivals.

📌 "The market is expanding" — Tyler Van Buren, analyst, TD Cowen

🔬 This Week's Deep Dive

Deep Dive · The Evidence Behind an Approval

Survival, precision, convenience.
Three drugs at the FDA's door in the same week

① Same review, different outcomes

All three drugs were trying to enter markets that already had treatments: ivonescimab against Keytruda in lung cancer, Wilserdly against Lilly and AstraZeneca pills in breast cancer, Livsayis against argenx and AstraZeneca drugs in myasthenia gravis. What separated them was the evidence. Ivonescimab had good PFS but weak OS and thin Western data. Wilserdly narrowed its patients to ESR1 mutations and made the effect unmistakable. Livsayis made convenience its edge. The FDA does not ask "does this look good?" but "is this evidence enough for these patients?"

③ Why it matters: approval strategy is set in trial design

These outcomes were decided years before the filings, when the trials were designed: which patients to enrol (ESR1-mutated), where to recruit (Asia or the West), which endpoint to make primary (PFS or OS), and what to claim against rivals (dosing interval). Ivonescimab is a strong, approved drug in China, but its data did not take the shape the US regulator wanted. Between a good drug and an approved drug sits a bridge called evidence design.

④ Career takeaway: the people who design the evidence

✓ Skill to build

The skill all three stories reward is turning the numbers in a paper into the language of approval and prescribing. In clinical development, that means knowing when PFS or OS should be the primary endpoint and what a hazard ratio and confidence interval actually say. In regulatory affairs, it means knowing which regions and patient mixes the FDA expects. In market access and marketing, it means showing how a biomarker or a dosing interval changes practice in the clinic. Bring one worked example to an interview and you stand out.

⚠ Trap

Predicting outcomes from labels like "Keytruda killer", "PFS benefit" or "first RNAi". Ivonescimab beat Keytruda in China and still missed the US bar, Wilserdly's biggest effect is in a defined subgroup, and Livsayis is approved but expected to build slowly. Read who was studied, on which endpoint, and in which countries.

💡 Editor's Insight

After these three stories I came away thinking an approval is less a prize for the drug than a test of its evidence. In the same week, one drug stopped short for lack of survival data, one went through quickly by narrowing its patients, and one opened the door with convenience. Next time you read an approval or a rejection, ask: in which patients, and on which endpoint? See you next issue. 🙏

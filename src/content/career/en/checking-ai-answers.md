I use AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity and Consensus when I study and when I prepare the newsletter. They're genuinely useful for getting my bearings quickly. But one thing I've learned while studying is that **even when an AI gives you a source, you still have to check the source is real**.

## The numbers

- One study checked the 115 references in 30 short medical papers written by ChatGPT (GPT-3.5): **47% did not exist**, and only **7%** were accurate [1].
- Another found that **55%** of GPT-3.5's citations and **18%** of GPT-4's were fabricated. Newer models do better, but the problem doesn't go away [2].

In biotech and pharma roles, this kind of mistake is costly. A non-existent paper or a wrong number in a regulatory document, a clinical report or a BD assessment can undo trust in one go.

## The four things I check

1. **Paper citations:** I open the DOI at `https://doi.org/`, or search PubMed if there's no DOI, then match the title, authors, year and journal.
2. **Clinical trials:** I search the NCT number on ClinicalTrials.gov and check that the primary outcome and comparator match what the AI said.
3. **Gene names:** I look up the official symbol on HGNC (genenames.org). Some old symbols were changed because of Excel, such as SEPT to SEPTIN [3].
4. **Numbers and dates:** I find them again in a primary source, such as the company's press release or the regulator's announcement.

The detailed steps, research sources and a practice exercise are in the free **[Checking AI Answers checklist](/en/resources)** in the resource library.

## What I've learned

I don't think the answer is to stop using AI. Keeping to the order **"AI drafts, I verify"** lets me work quickly and still produce something I can stand behind. I also looked at how to weigh the strength of evidence in [BIO:ON Insight Issue #16](/en/insight/2026-09-15).

If an interviewer asks "How do you use AI tools?", explaining **how you verify** will probably be far more convincing than listing which tools you use.

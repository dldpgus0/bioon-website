The most common line in biotech and pharma cover letters goes something like this:

> "The biotech industry has huge growth potential, and I have a strong interest in this field."

It isn't wrong — but anyone could write it, so it tells the reader nothing. What your motivation paragraph needs to show isn't **interest** but **understanding**. The easiest way to do that is to build it around one recent industry story.

## The four-sentence structure

1. **The story (what happened)** — one recent piece of news, with one number.
2. **The structure (why it matters)** — the industry dynamic behind the headline.
3. **The role (why this job exists)** — what the role you're applying for does within that dynamic.
4. **You (why you fit)** — one experience or skill that matches that role.

Keep this order within a single paragraph and it won't stop at a news summary — it flows naturally into you.

## Before and after

The news in each example is a real case covered in BIO:ON Insight. The experience in the last sentence is only an example — **replace it with your own**.

### CMC & quality

**Before:** "I believe quality is essential for biopharmaceuticals, so I'm applying for a quality role."

**After:** "Ultragenyx's gene therapy Fayuvi was rejected once over manufacturing, then approved by the FDA after the process was fixed — a clear sign that a drug's fate is decided as much where it's made as in the lab. Novartis committing $1.2B to its radiopharmaceutical supply chain points the same way. Even a drug with proven efficacy can't reach patients without proof it can be made to the same quality every time, and building that proof is the job of CMC and quality teams. Drawing on my undergraduate GMP practical, where I traced the root causes of logged deviations, I want to become someone who can explain quality issues with process data."

Source: [Issue #16](/en/insight/2026-09-22)

### Market access

**Before:** "I want to help patients access good medicines."

**After:** "What struck me about Lilly's oral obesity drug is that its slow early prescriptions weren't about efficacy — they were about delays in getting onto insurance formularies. It means that for a good drug to reach patients, value evidence and pricing strategy matter as much as clinical data. Reimbursement plays the same gatekeeping role in most health systems, and I want to contribute to translating clinical data into the language payers use. Calculating an ICER myself in a health economics assignment is where that started."

Source: [Issue #8](/en/insight/2026-07-19)

### Business development

**Before:** "I want to contribute to the growth of biotech companies expanding globally."

**After:** "Roche betting up to $2.5B on Hanmi's 'muscle-sparing' obesity candidate showed me that a smaller company's edge comes from data on a new axis, such as body composition, rather than from matching incumbents on efficacy. It also taught me that a deal's value lies in its upfront and milestone structure, not the headline total. I want to be a BD professional who presents differentiated data the way global partners need to see it, and reads deal terms closely. Analysing industry deals every week for a newsletter has given me the foundation to do that."

Source: [Issue #13](/en/insight/2026-09-01)

## Things to watch

- **One story only.** List several and you're back to being "someone very interested."
- **Get the numbers right.** Interviewers will ask about your cover letter. Save the source article and check it again before the interview.
- **Link it to the company if you can.** If the company's pipeline or business sits in the same trend, close with one line connecting them.
- **Don't overstate your experience.** The fourth sentence is what interviewers dig into most. Write only what you can explain in depth.

On a CV it's easy to write "keen interest in industry trends", but hard to write a line that actually shows it. So I took one deal from a recent newsletter issue and **practised turning the news I'd read into one line of analysis experience on a CV**.

## 1. What careers guides call a good line

The CV guide on Prospects, the UK graduate careers site, says [1]:

- Show **what you've accomplished**, not just your duties, and focus on **measurable outcomes** where possible.
- Replace stock phrases like "team player" or "hardworking" with **real examples**.
- **Don't exaggerate**, because you'll need to back up your claims at interview.
- **Mirror the keywords** in the job description, since many employers filter CVs with applicant tracking software.
- For graduates with little work history, it suggests a **skills-based CV** that puts skills ahead of employment history.

By that standard, I think an analysis you did for a newsletter or a course assignment can count as an achievement worth one line.

## 2. Practice material: two price tags on a drug that cuts injections

I read two stories from [BIO:ON Insight Issue #23](/en/insight/2026-11-03) and [Issue #24](/en/insight/2026-11-10) side by side.

**① BioMarin × GC Biopharma and Hanmi (announced 29 October 2026)**

- BioMarin took worldwide rights outside South Korea to HM15421/GC1134A, a candidate for Fabry disease [2][3].
- The deal is $70M upfront plus up to $405M in development and commercial milestones, so up to $475M in total, with royalties on top [2][3].
- Current Fabry enzyme replacement therapies are usually given by IV infusion every two weeks; this candidate aims for a **once-monthly subcutaneous injection**. It's in a Phase 1/2 trial [2][3].

**② CMS's final drug price negotiation rule (November 2026)**

- CMS has written the Medicare Drug Price Negotiation Program into regulation, applying from the 2029 price year [4].
- It identifies drugs for negotiation by **grouping all dosage forms and strengths with the same active ingredient and the same application holder** [4].
- Trade press read this as meaning subcutaneous versions of drugs such as Keytruda will be negotiated together with their IV versions [5][6].

**My own view:** Put side by side, the two stories show that the same "switch to subcutaneous" is valued differently as **a benefit to patients** and as **a way around price negotiation**. If I can explain that difference in one sentence, I think that's the "analysis" worth putting on a CV.

## 3. From news to one CV line, in four steps

1. **Change what I read into what I did:** write the action ("compared", "summarised", "calculated"), not "read articles".
2. **Give the scope in numbers:** only things I can count, such as how many deals, weeks or issues. If there's no outcome number, I write the scope and don't invent a result.
3. **Add one finding:** a short clause on what the analysis showed.
4. **Tune it to the job ad:** use the ad's own words, such as "deal structure" for BD or "pricing" for market access [1].

## 4. Before and after, by role

The "after" lines below are examples based on something I've actually done (summarising deal and regulatory news in a weekly newsletter). **Replace the numbers and activities with things you have really done.**

### BD

- **Before:** Keen interest in biotech licensing trends; read industry news regularly.
- **After:** Published 25 issues of a weekly biopharma newsletter, comparing the upfront and milestone structure of licensing deals; e.g. separated the guaranteed $70M upfront from up to $405M in conditional milestones in a subcutaneous Fabry deal.

### Market access and pricing

- **Before:** Strong interest in drug pricing policy.
- **After:** Summarised the 2026 final rule for Medicare drug price negotiation and explained to newsletter readers how grouping formulations of the same ingredient affects subcutaneous-switch strategies.

### Strategy, consulting or medical affairs

- **Before:** Analysed a range of industry issues.
- **After:** Linked one deal and one regulatory change on the same theme (easier dosing) into a one-page note on where "patient value" and "price value" diverge.

## 5. Checks after writing

- **Recheck every number against the source:** interviewers can ask about any number on a CV, so I keep the article links [1].
- **Call a maximum a maximum:** a deal's headline total is the most it could be worth if every condition is met, so I try not to mix it with guaranteed amounts.
- **Keep fact and interpretation apart:** reading the rule as covering subcutaneous versions is the trade press's interpretation, and I'd say so at interview too [5][6].
- **One message per line:** listing several deals turns me back into "someone who's interested".

## What I've learned

Trying to write one CV line turned into practice at explaining why two stories belong together. When I describe analysis experience, what matters most isn't an impressive verb but **what I actually did and a scope I can count**.

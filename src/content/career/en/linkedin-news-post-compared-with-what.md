On LinkedIn I often want to share an industry story. But a link with a few lines of summary underneath doesn't give readers anything they wouldn't get from the article itself.

University careers guides say much the same thing: when you share something, **add your own commentary or key takeaways** [1][2]. So what can I, as a student, add? While writing [BIO:ON Insight Issue #22](/en/insight/2026-10-27), I found one question that works well: **"What is this number being compared with?"**

## A four-line structure

1. **One line of fact:** what happened, with one number
2. **The comparison:** what that number is measured against (placebo, a rival drug, market expectations, the maximum value vs what's guaranteed)
3. **What it shows:** how the same story reads differently when you change the comparison
4. **Source link and one question:** the original article, plus a short question that invites comments

Only the first few lines of a LinkedIn post show before "see more", so I think lines 1 and 2 belong right at the top.

## Before and after (the three stories in Issue #22)

The "after" versions below are drafts I wrote myself from the Issue #22 stories. I rechecked every number against the source articles.

### 1. Celltrion × Kaigene, KG013

**Before:** "Celltrion has licensed Kaigene's KG013 antibody for up to $580.5M. Exciting times for Korean biotech's move into new drugs."

**After:**
> Celltrion has licensed Kaigene's preclinical antibody KG013 for "up to" $580.5M [3].
> That figure isn't guaranteed money: it's the maximum, adding the upfront and all the milestones together, and the upfront wasn't disclosed [3].
> So I've learned to read a deal like this by its conditions (what has to happen before money is paid) rather than by its headline value.
> When you see a deal story, which number do you check first?

### 2. AstraZeneca's Ultomiris, accelerated approval in IgA nephropathy

**Before:** "Ultomiris has been approved by the FDA in IgA nephropathy. That's its fifth indication."

**After:**
> AstraZeneca's Ultomiris won accelerated FDA approval in IgA nephropathy, its fifth US indication [4][5].
> The key number is a 43.4% reduction in proteinuria at week 34, and that's measured against placebo [5].
> It's also an *accelerated* approval, granted on proteinuria as a surrogate marker. Whether it preserves kidney function will be judged on the week-106 eGFR result [5].
> I've got into the habit of asking what an approval was actually based on.

### 3. AbbVie's lutikizumab phase 3

**Before:** "AbbVie's lutikizumab met its primary endpoint in a phase 3 hidradenitis suppurativa trial."

**After:**
> AbbVie's lutikizumab met its primary endpoint in a phase 3 trial in hidradenitis suppurativa: 31.4% reached HiSCR75 at week 16, versus 23.4% on placebo [6].
> Against placebo it's a win. Against what the market expected, analysts said the gap was small [6].
> Shares in rival Avalo fell 35% in early trading [6].
> For me it showed how the same result gets a very different grade depending on what you compare it with.

## Things to watch

- **Recheck numbers in the original.** Qualifiers like "up to", "versus placebo" and "in early trading" change the meaning if you drop them.
- **Always link the source.** LinkedIn posts get screenshotted and passed around, so I think the evidence should travel with them.
- **Don't pretend to be an expert.** Phrases like "I've learned" or "I think" read more naturally from someone who's studying the field. I also avoid anything that sounds like investment advice.
- **One question only.** A single short question is enough to invite comments.

## What I've learned

AI can summarise a news story in seconds, but asking "compared with what?" is something a person who's studying the field can add. The same question works in interviews too: I practised adding the comparison right after the number in [my post on interview follow-up questions](/en/career/rule-change-follow-up-questions).

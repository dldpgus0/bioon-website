## What this guide is for

News stories compress trial results and approval decisions into a few lines. This guide shows you how to find and read **the primary sources** yourself. In an RA, clinical or medical interview, "I checked the original record" is a sentence that sets you apart.

It covers four places:

1. **ClinicalTrials.gov** — the global registry and results database for clinical trials
2. **Drugs@FDA and Novel Drug Approvals** — US approval histories and FDA review documents
3. **EMA EPARs** — public assessment reports for medicines approved in the EU
4. **MFDS Drug Safety Nara (의약품안전나라)** — Korea's approval and clinical trial authorisation data

Websites change often. The menu names below follow each site's official help pages, which you'll find in the Sources at the end.

## 1. ClinicalTrials.gov — reading a trial record

### Finding a study

- Search by drug name, condition or trial name (such as a code name you saw in a news story, like SERENA-6) [1].
- To see only studies that have posted results, follow the official guide to finding studies with results [2].
- Every study has a unique **identifier starting with NCT**. If an article, paper or press release gives the NCT number, it takes you straight to the right record.

### The record's tabs [3]

- **Study Details** — the study's purpose, design, participants and sites
- **Researcher View** — the same information arranged for researchers and journal editors
- **Results Posted** — the results tab, shown only when results have been submitted
- **Record History** — a table of changes made to the record over time

### Check these first

- **Primary Outcome Measure** — what the trial uses to judge success
- **Arms** — what it compared against: placebo or standard of care?
- **Population and size** — which patients, and how many
- **Status and dates** — recruiting or completed, and when results are due

### Reading the results tab [4]

Results come as tables that usually include:

- the participant groups and their characteristics (such as average age — nothing that identifies individuals)
- the findings for each outcome measure
- a summary of adverse events

**Results don't include conclusions or interpretations** [4]. Interpretation comes from papers, conference presentations and press releases — which is why it's worth practising telling the raw data apart from the spin. Results are usually submitted no later than a year after the study ends [4].

**Editor's tip:** check the Record History to see whether the primary outcome changed partway through. Thinking about why it changed makes good interview material.

## 2. FDA — approval histories and review documents

### Novel Drug Approvals

The FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) publishes a yearly list of **novel drugs** it has approved [5]. It's the quickest way to see what's new this year. Note that vaccines, blood products and **cell and gene therapies** — products regulated by CBER — are not on this list [5].

### Finding review documents in Drugs@FDA

1. Search Drugs@FDA by **brand name**. The search is strict — even an extra space after the name can stop it finding the drug [6].
2. On the product page, expand **"Approval Date(s) and History, Letters, Labels, Reviews for NDA/BLA…"** [6].
3. In the table, use the **Letters, Labels and Review** columns on the right [6].
   - **Label** — the approved prescribing information (indications, dosing, warnings)
   - **Review** — the FDA's review documents, which may include a Summary Review, Medical Review, Chemistry Review and Pharmacology Review [6]
4. Some confidential information may be redacted, but there's usually enough to understand the review [6].

**Start with the Summary Review (editor's tip).** It's the shortest account of why the FDA approved the drug and what concerns it had.

### Reading expedited-programme labels

Fast Track, Breakthrough Therapy, Accelerated Approval and Priority Review are the FDA's expedited programmes [7]. **Accelerated Approval** in particular grants approval on a surrogate endpoint and requires confirmatory trials, so more data has to follow after approval [7].

## 3. EMA EPARs — European public assessment reports

The EMA publishes an **EPAR (European Public Assessment Report)** for every centrally authorised medicine [8]. An EPAR typically includes [8]:

- a plain-language summary (in question-and-answer form) and the package leaflet
- efficacy, safety and pharmacokinetic data, and the mechanism of action
- product information, the risk management plan, the scientific discussion, and procedural steps before and after authorisation

You can filter by therapeutic area or type (orphan, generic, biosimilar and so on), and also find medicines that were refused or withdrawn [8].

**Editor's tip:** read the FDA review and the EPAR for the same drug side by side to see how two regulators weighed the same data.

## 4. MFDS Drug Safety Nara — Korean approvals and trials

Korea's MFDS runs Drug Safety Nara (의약품안전나라), where you can search [9]:

- **Medicine search** — product and company information for medicines approved in Korea
- **Clinical trial authorisations** — trials authorised in Korea, searchable by criteria

If you're preparing for RA or clinical roles in Korea, check **which trials of a drug you care about have been authorised in Korea**.

## 5. Practice — follow one news story back to its sources

Try it with **camizestrant**, covered in BIO:ON Insight Issue #15. The FDA granted accelerated approval on 4 September 2026 even though its advisory committee had voted 6–3 against [10].

1. Search ClinicalTrials.gov for the pivotal trial, **SERENA-6**, and note its primary outcome and comparator.
2. From the FDA announcement, note the scope of approval — which patients, in which combination [10].
3. Find what the advisory committee was worried about in the coverage [11].
4. Put the three together in one paragraph: "approved on what evidence, despite which concerns, under what conditions."

That paragraph is the skeleton of an answer to "Tell me about a recent approval you followed" in an RA or medical interview.
